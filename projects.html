RNA Topology Benchmark
ML-Projects · Topological Data Analysis · Computational Biology
What this is
A novel benchmarking framework for RNA structure prediction that introduces topological metrics – persistent homology, Betti numbers, and a custom Topological Quality Score (TQS) – to evaluate how well predicted structures capture the true topological features of RNA molecules. Traditional metrics like RMSD miss structural invariants; this framework doesn't.
Persistent Homology Betti Numbers Persistent Laplacian TDA RNA Structure Python
Context
RNA structure prediction has advanced rapidly with deep learning models like RhoFold, AlphaFold 3, and ARES, yet evaluation remains stuck in the RMSD era. Root-mean-square deviation measures geometric distance between atoms but ignores whether the predicted structure preserves the topological features of the ground truth — the loops, cavities, and higher-dimensional holes that define RNA function. A prediction can score well on RMSD while missing an entire stem-loop. For RNA therapeutics — mRNA vaccines, antisense oligonucleotides, riboswitches — getting the topology right is often more important than getting the coordinates right.
My Contribution
I developed a benchmarking framework that builds persistence diagrams from both predicted and ground-truth RNA structures, then compares them using bottleneck and Wasserstein distances across homological dimensions. The framework introduces three novel metrics: a Topological Quality Score (TQS) that aggregates topological fidelity into a single number, a Topological Complexity Index (TCI) that quantifies the inherent structural richness of each molecule, and the persistent Laplacian spectral gap (λ₁) — a continuous measure of topological connectivity more sensitive than discrete Betti numbers alone. The full pipeline is implemented in Python with reproducible notebooks and public data.
Why It Matters
These metrics are invariant to rigid-body transformations, robust to small perturbations, and capture structural features that are invisible to geometric metrics. They provide the mathematical tools needed to evaluate whether an RNA structure prediction model is getting the biology right, not just the geometry. This work sits at the intersection of topological data analysis and computational biology — a space I believe is underexplored and full of potential for pharma applications.

Dragon Hatchling Architecture
ML-Theory · Biologically-Inspired Neural Networks · Architecture Analysis
What this is
An independent implementation, analysis, and extension of the Baby Dragon Hatchling (BDH) architecture – a biologically-inspired neural network that bridges transformers and models of the brain through scale-free, neuron-level graph interactions with emergent attention mechanisms.
BDH Architecture Scale-Free Networks Emergent Attention Hebbian Learning Sparse Computation PyTorch
Context
The Dragon Hatchling architecture (Kosowski et al., 2025) is one of the most interesting post-transformer proposals in recent memory. It reimagines neural computation by scaling in a high-dimensional neuron dimension rather than the latent dimension of standard transformers. Parameters are localized to neuron pairs, mirroring biological connectivity. Attention emerges naturally from sparse graph-based interactions rather than being explicitly computed — a fundamental shift in how we think about information flow in neural networks. The architecture matches GPT-2–scale transformers on language and translation tasks while retaining full causal interpretability at the neuron level.
My Contribution
This repository provides an independent review, implementation, and analysis of the BDH-GPU architecture. I explore its key design choices in depth: shared-weight layers that act as "learned programs" (the causal circuit Gx and output circuit Gy), non-negative ReLU activations for biological plausibility, and the constrained Q=K attention mechanism with causal masking. The implementation includes interpretability tools for visualizing the emergent hub structure and signal propagation dynamics. My review critically examines BDH's claims through the lens of both computational neuroscience (my PhD background) and practical AI engineering.
Why It Matters
BDH demonstrates that biologically plausible architectures can follow transformer-like scaling laws while maintaining full causal interpretability. This has direct implications for AI governance: if we can trace how a model reaches a decision through its neuron-level graph dynamics, we can build genuinely auditable AI systems. Understanding these bridges between neuroscience and deep learning is central to my work in AI assurance for regulated industries — a model you can explain is a model you can govern.

Bayesian Governance
Audit-Risk · Bayesian Inference · Enterprise Risk Monitoring
What this is
A mathematical framework for continuous governance monitoring using Bayesian Beta-Binomial models. Instead of binary pass/fail audit judgments, this system maintains calibrated probability distributions over control effectiveness, updates them with each new observation, and provides principled early-warning signals for governance deterioration.
Beta-Binomial Bayesian Inference Shannon Entropy Monte Carlo Governance Python
Context
Traditional audit reporting operates on a cycle of months or quarters. A control is either "effective" or "deficient" — a binary judgment delivered long after the underlying reality has changed. In regulated industries like pharma, where a single compliance failure can affect patient safety, this latency is unacceptable. The audit profession has discussed risk-based approaches for decades, but the mathematical tooling has lagged behind: most frameworks still rely on static heatmaps, ordinal risk ratings, and threshold-based alerts that lack probabilistic grounding.
My Contribution
I developed a complete mathematical framework that models each control as a Beta-Binomial process: the prior encodes historical performance, and each new observation (finding, exception, test result) updates the posterior distribution. Shannon entropy of the posterior serves as the monitoring signal — rising entropy means increasing uncertainty about control effectiveness, which is itself a leading indicator of governance breakdown. Simulations demonstrate a 114-day early detection advantage over traditional threshold-based methods. The framework extends to Monte Carlo stress-testing with strategic adversary modeling (what happens if an intelligent adversary targets the weakest controls?) and Jensen's inequality-based antifragility metrics (which controls actually improve under stress?). All simulation code is public, and the theoretical foundations were published as a six-part series on the Audit 2.0 Substack.
Why It Matters
This is the mathematical backbone of the AI Assurance argument I'm building at Roche. If audit can move from "we checked last quarter and it was fine" to "the posterior probability of control effectiveness is 0.87 and falling, with entropy crossing the alert threshold in approximately three weeks," then assurance becomes predictive rather than reactive. It's the difference between a smoke detector and a fire investigation. The framework is designed to be implemented on top of existing audit data — no new data collection required, just a new way of thinking about what the data already tells us.

ClinOps AI
ML-Projects · Clinical Trial Safety Intelligence · Deep Learning · CDISC
What this is
An end-to-end deep learning pipeline for clinical trial safety intelligence, built on real FDA-grade CDISC data (254 Alzheimer's patients, PHUSE pilot study). From SAS binary parsing through Pydantic validation, DuckDB analytics, 9 publication-quality figures, and an 8-model ablation study – including a 14-page LaTeX technical report.
CDISC / SDTM Clinical Safety Deep Learning GRU + Attention Integrated Gradients MC Dropout DuckDB Python
Context
Clinical trial safety monitoring generates massive volumes of structured adverse event, lab, and vital sign data in CDISC/SDTM format. Data Safety Monitoring Boards (DSMBs) need to detect safety signals early, but traditional statistical methods struggle with the multivariate, longitudinal, and often imbalanced nature of clinical safety data. Meanwhile, deep learning papers in clinical AI routinely report impressive benchmarks on large datasets — but pharma trials often have N=200–500 patients in early phases. The question nobody asks honestly: at what sample size does deep learning actually outperform simpler methods?
My Contribution
I built an end-to-end pipeline that ingests SAS binary transport files (no SAS license required), validates through Pydantic schemas, loads into DuckDB for analytics, and trains 8 models in a rigorous ablation study: from regularized logistic regression through random forests, gradient boosting, MLP, LSTM, GRU, GRU+Attention, and a Conditional VAE. Feature attribution uses Integrated Gradients; uncertainty quantification uses MC Dropout. Nine publication-quality figures are generated automatically, including a DSMB-ready safety dashboard. The pipeline runs on real FDA-grade CDISC data from the PHUSE pilot study (254 Alzheimer's patients). A data leakage bug was caught and fixed mid-development and documented transparently in the report. The CVAE overfitting anomaly was explicitly flagged rather than hidden.
Why It Matters
The key finding is one many papers would bury: at N=254, regularized logistic regression outperforms most neural models. The GRU+Attention model showed promise on temporal patterns but couldn't overcome the sample size constraint. This intellectual honesty is the point — in regulated environments, knowing when not to use deep learning is as important as knowing how. The pipeline also demonstrates that modern Python tooling (Pydantic, DuckDB, PyTorch) can replace legacy SAS-dependent workflows entirely, lowering the barrier to rigorous safety analytics in smaller organizations and academic clinical research.
